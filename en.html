    Timeline

    The whole topic at a glance. Once these dates are in your head, the rest falls into place.

    1. 1890sRagtime is popular. Black pianists, mainly on the piano. Scott Joplin.
    2. late 19th – early 20th centuryJazz is born in the USA, above all in New Orleans.
    3. 1915The word “jazz” appears in Chicago.
    4. 1917Storyville is closed, the heyday of New Orleans jazz ends and musicians move to Chicago. In the same year the Original Dixieland Jazz Band makes the first jazz recording.
    5. 1920sThe peak of Chicago jazz. Soloists and the saxophone come to the fore.
    6. early 1930sSwing and big bands arrive. Dixieland's golden age ends.
    7. late 1940s – early 1950sDixieland makes a comeback and is still popular today.

    What makes jazz jazz?

    Jazz emerged in the USA in the late 19th and early 20th century, above all in New Orleans. The word “jazz” appeared in Chicago in 1915.

    Four key featuresImprovisation · tone colour (timbre) · instrumental line-ups · rhythm, melody and harmony

    Where cultures meet

    • African influence: making music collectively, improvisation, call and response (a lead singer and a group answering).
    • European influence: harmony and instruments.
    • Caribbean influence: rhythm-based dance music, more exotic and complex rhythmic patterns.

    Social background and the legacy of slavery

    • It was born in African American communities.
    • Jazz was a means of self-expression and of resisting social restrictions.
    • During slavery and racial segregation, work songs and spirituals helped people get through hard working lives and oppression.

    The cradle of jazz: New Orleans

    • A multicultural population where different musical styles and traditions mixed.
    • Social and racial restrictions were looser there.
    • Congo Square was a square where people played music, danced and kept their cultural heritage alive at weekends.
    • Funeral procession bands are one of the foundations of jazz.

    Ragtime and blues

    The two styles jazz grew out of.

    Ragtime

    • One of the first styles, and a strong influence on how jazz developed.
    • Popular in the 1890s.
    • Played by Black pianists, mainly on the piano.
    • Lively, danceable music that laid the foundations for jazz.
    Scott Joplin 1868–1917American pianist, creator of ragtime

    “Maple Leaf Rag”, “The Entertainer”, “Pleasant Moments”. 44 pieces, one ballet, two operas. His music was rediscovered in the early 1970s.

    Blues

    • A central style in the making of jazz.
    • Based on African American work songs and spirituals.
    • 12-bar structure, solo singing, improvisation.
    • Sang about personal pain, loss and joy.
    • Key artists: Bessie Smith, Muddy Waters, B.B. King.

    Terms and names

    • Collective improvisation: the players all play at the same time, but each one improvises independently.
    • Jelly Roll Morton was one of the first jazz composers and pianists.
    • Louis Armstrong was a 1920s trumpeter associated with swing.

    New Orleans jazz

    Rough, raw, intense and emotional. Its heyday lasted until 1917.

    Musical roots

    • African American rhythms and singing styles.
    • Work songs, spirituals and the blues.
    • European marches and dance music, mixed with ragtime and boogie-woogie. The result was a hybrid music.

    Amateur bands

    • They played in the entertainment venues of the Storyville district. There were nearly 30 bands.
    • They improvised by ear.
    • They sounded like marching and circus bands.

    Rhythm

    1234

    A march-like pulse: the accent is on beats 1 and 3. There's no off-beat, i.e. no accent on beats 2 and 4.

    Band

    • Small bands, more chances to perform.
    • Front line (melody section): cornet or trumpet, trombone, clarinet.
    • Rhythm section: tuba, banjo, drums.
    • Later the tuba was replaced by the double bass, the banjo by the guitar, and the piano was added.
    • The music wasn't written down. It was created on the spot from a set theme: a march or church melody, a ragtime piece or a 12-bar blues.

    Best-known musicians

    Joe “King” Oliver 1885–1938Cornet and trumpet player

    Led New Orleans' most influential band, King Oliver's Creole Jazz Band.

    Jelly Roll Morton 1890–1941Ragtime pianist, composer, bandleader

    Laid the foundations of classic jazz with his recordings with the Red Hot Peppers.

    Louis Armstrong 1900–1971

    • The brightest star of New Orleans jazz: cornet player, trumpeter, pianist, arranger and composer.
    • With him, jazz became soloists' music.
    • A role model in everything: personality, inventiveness, emotion and polished technique.
    • Brought in chords and written notation. Added Black church music, New Orleans and African rhythms.
    • Brought blue notes into the music.
    • A raspy vocal timbre and scat singing (improvised singing with syllables instead of words).
    • Pieces: “What a Wonderful World”, “West End Blues”, “Hotter Than Hot”, “I'm in the Mood for Love”.
    Without Armstrong there would be no jazz, and without jazz there would be no modern pop music or rock.Joachim-Ernst Berendt, “Das Jazzbuch”

    The end

    In 1917 the Storyville entertainment district was closed and musicians headed to Chicago.

    Dixieland

    The New Orleans style as played by white musicians. Jazz brings races together.

    Papa Jack LaineBandleader and drummer, creator of Dixieland

    His band often beat Black musicians in the bands' street battles.

    Nick LaRoccaLeader of the Original Dixieland Jazz Band

    The band made the first jazz recordings in 1917.

    A changed sound

    • The sound was less powerful.
    • The white musicians had better playing technique.
    • The line-up stayed the same.
    • The music was faster and the melody smoother.
    • The tone colours were less intense.
    • The typical sound came from a solo instrument, the trumpet or clarinet.

    Recording and spread

    • In 1917 the Original Dixieland Jazz Band made the first jazz recordings.
    • For the first time jazz pieces became famous, and the record sold over a million copies.
    • Imitators sprang up all over America.

    Well-known bands

    • Original Dixieland Jazz Band
    • New Orleans Rhythm Kings
    • Louis Armstrong & All Stars Orchestra

    Heyday and comeback

    • Its golden age ended in the early 1930s with the arrival of swing and big bands.
    • In the late 1940s and early 1950s people went back to Dixieland.
    • Still popular today.

    Chicago jazz

    The new centre of jazz. Collective improvisation gave way to solo improvisation. Chicago jazz is jazz played by white musicians.

    The blues blends with jazz

    • Its peak was in the 1920s.
    • It expressed the problems caused by racial discrimination.
    • Bluesy jazz got white high-school students playing.
    • The Austin High School Gang was a breeding ground for young talent and the new style.

    A more demanding sound

    • White musicians added technical polish, harmonic complexity and arrangements.
    • The solo became more important and the player's virtuosity came to the fore.
    • Break: the band goes silent while the soloist improvises.
    • Collective solos were kept only at the start and end of a piece.
    • The march-like rhythm was replaced by a smoother one. The influence of the blues was stronger than that of ragtime.
    1234

    Compare with New Orleans: the march-like accent on beats 1 and 3 is gone, and the pulse is more even and smoother.

    The arrival of the saxophone

    • The saxophone came into use with the Chicago style and later became the symbol of jazz.
    • The first saxophone virtuoso was tenor saxophonist Bud Freeman.

    Sections in dialogue

    Fletcher Henderson 1897–1952Pianist, bandleader

    Split the band into sections and created a new band style by contrasting timbres. Worked out the principles of arranging for a large band.

    Coleman Hawkins 1904–1969Tenor saxophonist

    Played in Henderson's band.

    The success of dance bands

    • Their repertoire included jazz arrangements of pop songs.
    • The most popular dance band leader was Paul Whiteman 1890–1967.
    • His band included cornet player Bix Beiderbecke and guitarist Eddie Lang.

    Duke Ellington 1899–1974

    • Composer, pianist and bandleader.
    • Worked mainly in New York, at the Cotton Club in Harlem.
    • His works grew out of improvising with the band: a piece started with a piano solo and the other musicians joined in.
    • Jungle style: expressive sounds from the trumpet and trombone.
    • Pieces: “Mood Indigo”, “It Don't Mean a Thing”.

    Concert review

    Between September and February you go to one concert or musical production and give an oral review of it with your group.

    PresentationLength 7–10 minutes. Every group member talks about their own opinion of the concert (the main part).

    1. Introduction

    • When and where the concert took place
    • Who performed
    • Which pieces were performed
    • A couple of general sentences about the mood of the concert or how the programme was put together

    2. Main part: the review

    • Start with the pieces that were performed. You can find interesting facts and quotes about the composers or use the programme.
    • Don't quote the programme word for word or copy it onto your slides. Take one interesting idea or fact and use it to give your own thoughts and conclusions.
    • Most of the main part has to be your thoughts on the performers and the performances. Questions to start from are on the “Analysis questions” tab.

    3. Conclusion

    Three or four sentences that include:

    • the conclusions you reached in your discussion;
    • whether the music, as played by this performer, made you think (about what?), thrilled you (why?) or bored you (why?);
    • a comparison with concerts you've been to before;
    • suggestions for the performers or organisers.

    Analysis questions

    Use these questions when you're working out what to say about the performers and the performance. For every answer, explain why you think so.

    Performers

    • Who are they? Use the programme or the internet. If there's a lot of material, pick the important things: competition wins, performances in famous concert halls, work with well-known musicians.
    • Was their playing or singing of a high standard, or was it lacking? Why?

    The performance and your impression

    • How does the performer perform the piece?
    • What emotions did it bring out in you?
    • How did the piece affect you personally? Did it bring up thoughts, feelings or memories?

    Composer

    • Did the performance bring out the composer's style and way of thinking?
    • What do you think of this composer's music, and how do you think it should be performed?

    Verdict

    Was the performance

    • original and exceptional,
    • strange and hard to understand,
    • or a disappointment?

    Make sure you give reasons.

    Opera or balletAlso talk about the staging, the sets, the costumes and the choreography.

    Tips

    Teacher's tips

    • Be honest but objective. Express your feelings, but back them up with specific examples.
    • Avoid general phrases like “it was good”. Explain why it was good.
    • Speak clearly and logically so your listeners can follow.
    • Pay attention to correct language, but don't be afraid of figurative expressions and your own personal style.
    • Check the names of performers and composers in the programme. If there's no programme, use ENE (the Estonian encyclopedia), a music dictionary or, as a last resort, the internet.

    I'd also suggest

    • Take the programme with you or take a photo of it. It has the names, pieces and facts you'll need later.
    • Make notes straight after the concert while your impressions are fresh: what you liked, what surprised you, what stuck with you.
    • Agree early in your group who will talk about what, so the same points don't get repeated.
    • Don't read from your slides. Put keywords and pictures on the slides and say the rest in your own words.
    • Do a practice run and time it. 7–10 minutes fills up faster than you'd think.

    What UPT is

    A written work you do alone, in a pair or in a group, together with a supervisor. You defend it in front of a panel.

    Everything here is a summary of the school's UPT guide (PDF).

    Three forms

    • Research paper: you investigate a problem and find answers to your research questions.
    • Practical project: you create something (for example an app, a game, an event or a work of art) and analyse your working process.
    • Student company: a written paper and an activity report.

    Types of research paper

    • Based on a survey
    • Based on interviews
    • Based on an experiment or observation
    • Based on text or video analysis
    • Local history
    • Career research

    Basic rules

    • The paper is written in Estonian and in an academic style: clear, precise and neutral. One sentence is one idea, one paragraph develops one line of thought.
    • A paper with several authors is bigger, and each author's contribution has to be clear. All authors present.
    • The supervisor is a member of school staff. They can say no if they already supervise too many students or the topic isn't their field.
    • Help is available from the Moodle course “Uurimistöö alused” (Research basics), your supervisor, the writing camps and the seminars.

    Your responsibilities

    • Agree on regular meetings with your supervisor and stick to the deadlines.
    • You're responsible for making sure your data is correct.
    • Your supervisor and the UPT coordinator must have access to your materials (OneDrive).
    • The work must not be plagiarised and must be formatted correctly.

    Sensitive topics

    Avoid sensitive data (health, including mental health, ethnic origin, biometrics etc.) and topics that divide society (religion, politics). If you still want a topic like that, you need the consent of your supervisor, the school management and the UPT coordinator.

    Structure of the paper

    The parts always come in this order. The table of contents, headings and page numbers must all match.

    Research paper

    1. Title page
    2. Author's declaration
    3. Table of contents
    4. List of abbreviations used (if needed)
    5. Introduction: topic, problem, aim and research questions or hypotheses
    6. Theoretical background: what the sources say about the topic, with key terms explained
    7. Methodology: how and whom you studied, and why you did it that way
    8. Results and discussion
    9. Conclusion: answers to the research questions
    10. References
    11. Appendices

    Practical project

    1. Title page
    2. Author's declaration
    3. Table of contents
    4. Introduction
    5. Theoretical background
    6. Description of the process and result: what you did and how
    7. Analysis of the working process and self-analysis: what went well, what didn't, what you learned
    8. Conclusion
    9. References
    10. Appendices

    Summary

    It comes with a summary in Estonian and English. The templates are in appendix 3 (Estonian) and appendix 4 (English) of the UPT guide.

    Formatting

    The most important thing is that the whole paper is consistent. Set up your styles right at the start, then the table of contents is generated automatically.

    Source: PERGi kirjalike tööde vormistamise juhend (PERG guide to formatting written work, PDF).

    SoftwareThe paper is written in MS Word (Microsoft 365 is free for students) and saved to OneDrive so your supervisor can see it.

    Writing rules

    • Single-digit numbers in words (two points), larger ones in figures (120 companies). Always use figures with a unit of measurement (5 cm).
    • Years in figures, not “last year”.
    • Choose one form and stick to it throughout: impersonal (it was studied), we-form (we studied) or I-form (I studied). The same goes for tense.
    • A foreign-language term goes in brackets in italics the first time: enesetõhusus (self-efficacy).
    • A punctuation mark goes straight after the word, then a space. Brackets and quotation marks sit tight against the text.

    Referencing

    PERG uses the APA 7 system: the text gives the author's name and the year, and the full entry goes in the “References” chapter.

    • More detailed examples are in the referencing chapter of the formatting guide.
    • Cite everything whose ideas or data you use, including pictures and charts. Only common knowledge doesn't need a citation.
    • Paraphrasing is the main method: you sum up an idea in your own words and add a citation, for example (Covington, 2000).
    • A quotation goes in quotation marks and ends with a citation. Omitted parts are marked with … or [---].
    • Presenting a quotation as a paraphrase is plagiarism, even if there's a citation.
    • Wikipedia is not used as a source. Use the original sources listed at the bottom of the article.
    • Lifestyle magazines, entertainment sites and advertising leaflets aren't suitable sources (unless they're what you're studying).
    • Use Zotero (zotero.org): it creates citations and the reference list automatically.
    Example“Analysing the collected data […] is of decisive importance in research.” (Hirsjärvi et al., 2010, p. 206)

    Using AI

    You may only use AI if you cite it and describe how you used it: what you asked, what answer you got and how you changed it.

    The rules are on page 8 of the UPT guide.

    Generally allowed

    • Asking for suggestions to find sources
    • Finding synonyms for search terms
    • Getting a complicated theory explained to you
    • Summarising a long text for a first overview (but not as your own text)
    • Translating
    • Help with interpreting statistics, charts and results
    • Finding programming bugs
    • Editing your own text (shortening it, removing repeated words)
    • Improving formatting and structure

    Not allowed

    • Generating long passages of text and passing them off as your own
    • Presenting information you haven't checked
    • Making up or falsifying data
    • Giving AI research data that contains personal data
    • Giving AI other people's unpublished work
    RememberPresenting AI text as your own ideas is academic fraud.

    Tips that save time

    These aren't requirements from the guide, just suggestions that make life easier.

    • Set up your Word styles on day one. If the headings and body text are right from the start, the table of contents takes one click.
    • Add sources to Zotero as soon as you read them. Hunting for sources at the end is the most annoying part.
    • After every meeting with your supervisor, write down what you agreed and when the next deadline is.
    • Read the feedback guides (appendix 9 of the UPT guide for research papers and appendix 10 for practical projects). They spell out exactly what earns points at the seminar and the defence.
    • Pin down your aim and research questions early. Without them your supervisor can't help you and the theory section loses focus.
    • For a practical project, keep a development diary: what you did, what didn't work and why you changed things. Then the self-analysis is easy to write.
    • Don't leave everything for the writing camp. The camp is good for polishing, not for starting from scratch.
    • Practise your defence speech in front of someone and think about what the panel might ask.
